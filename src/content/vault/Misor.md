## Introduction

Misor (Μισωρ) was a Phoenician deity whose name embodies the concept of straightness, uprightness, and justice. Known primarily through the writings of Philo of Byblos, who preserved the theogony of the Phoenician priest Sanchuniathon, Misor appears as one half of a divine pair alongside [[Sydyk]] (Righteousness). Together, they represent the foundational moral principles of the Phoenician cosmological order and are credited with the practical discovery of salt, a substance essential to preservation and purification in the ancient world.

---

## Etymology

- **Root meaning**: The name Misor derives from the West Semitic root *y-sh-r* (√yšr), meaning "straight," "upright," or "just"
- **Language of origin**: Phoenician (Northwest Semitic)
- **Variants and evolution**: The Greek rendering Μισωρ (Misor) preserves the Phoenician pronunciation. In Akkadian, the cognate deity is known as Mīšaru; in Mari, the equivalent is Mešar
- **Symbolic implications**: The concept of "straightness" carries moral weight, suggesting integrity, honesty, and the correct path—both literally and metaphorically. The pairing of the roots *y-sh-r* (straight/just) and *ṣ-d-q* (righteous) is attested in Phoenician and Ugaritic texts as a common hendiadys expressing "righteousness and justice"

> _"They discovered how to use salt. These came Misor and Sydyk, that is, Agile and Just."_  
> — Philo of Byblos, *Phoenician History* (preserved by Eusebius)

## Myths and Legends

**The Discovery of Salt**  
According to the Phoenician theogony preserved by Philo of Byblos, Misor and his companion [[Sydyk]] were the first to discover the use of salt. This discovery was not merely practical but held cosmological significance—salt preserved food, purified offerings, and sealed covenants. In the ancient world, salt was often associated with incorruptibility and eternal truth, making its discoverers fitting personifications of justice and righteousness.

**Birth from the Titans**  
Misor and [[Sydyk]] are described as sons of [[Amunos]] and [[Magos]], who were themselves born from the Wanderers or Titans. This lineage places them in the early generations of the Phoenician cosmogony, emerging from primordial forces to establish moral order in the cosmos.

**Father of Taautus**  
Misor is credited as the father of [[Taautus]], who is identified with the Egyptian [[Thoth]] and Greek [[Hermes]]. Taautus is celebrated as the inventor of the first written alphabet, suggesting that Misor's principle of "straightness" extends to the ordering of language and communication itself.

> _"From Misor was born Taautus, who invented the first written alphabet."_  
> — Philo of Byblos, *Phoenician History*

## Worship and Rituals

- **Forms of worship**: Specific cultic practices dedicated to Misor are not well documented in surviving sources. As an abstract personification, Misor may have been honored more as a principle invoked in legal and ethical contexts rather than through temple cult
- **Festivals**: No specific festivals are attested
- **Sacred spaces**: No dedicated temples to Misor are known from archaeological evidence
- **Archaeological remains**: The name appears primarily in literary sources rather than epigraphic evidence, suggesting Misor functioned more as a theological concept than an object of popular cult worship

---

## Familial Relationships

**Parents**: [[Amunos]] (father) and [[Magos]] (mother), themselves descended from the Titans or Wanderers

**Sibling**: [[Sydyk]] (Righteousness)—the two form an inseparable pair in Phoenician cosmogony

**Offspring**: [[Taautus]], the legendary inventor of writing, equated with [[Thoth]] and [[Hermes]]

**Divine Partnership**: Misor's constant association with [[Sydyk]] suggests they function as complementary aspects of cosmic justice—straightness/uprightness (Misor) and righteousness/judgment (Sydyk)

---

## Later Influence

- **Syncretism**: Misor's Mesopotamian cognate is [[Mīšaru]], who was similarly paired with Kittu (equivalent to [[Sydyk]]). In the city of Mari, the equivalent deities Išar and Mešar are found. This suggests a widespread West Semitic and Mesopotamian theological tradition of paired justice deities
- **Legacy in later religions**: The pairing of justice concepts (*mishpat* and *tzedek* in Hebrew) becomes fundamental to biblical theology, though the personified deities themselves are not worshipped in monotheistic tradition
- **Presence in modern literature and culture**: Misor appears primarily in academic discussions of Phoenician religion and comparative mythology

---

## Modern Interpretations

- **Psychological/archetypal readings**: Misor and [[Sydyk]] can be understood as archetypal representations of humanity's innate moral sense—the recognition of straightness/correctness and righteousness as fundamental ordering principles
- **Comparative mythology**: The pairing of justice deities is found across the ancient Near East:
  - Mesopotamian: [[Mīšaru]] and Kittu
  - Mari: Mešar and Išar  
  - Ugaritic: References to *yšr* and *ṣdq* as paired concepts
  - Hebrew Bible: *mishpat* (justice/judgment) and *tzedek* (righteousness) as complementary virtues
- **Recent academic theories**: Scholars note that Misor and [[Sydyk]] represent the abstraction and personification of ethical concepts in Phoenician thought, suggesting a sophisticated theological development where moral principles achieve divine status

---

## Connection to Other Deities

**Mesopotamian Cognates**: The Akkadian deity [[Mīšaru]] shares both etymology and function with Misor, and like Misor, is paired with a righteousness deity (Kittu, cognate to [[Sydyk]])

**Egyptian Syncretism**: Through his son [[Taautus]] (identified with [[Thoth]]), Misor connects to the Egyptian tradition of cosmic order (*ma'at*) and divine wisdom

**Greek Interpretations**: Greek sources sometimes render Misor as "Well-freed" (εὐλυτρωμένος), suggesting liberation or release alongside the primary meaning of straightness
