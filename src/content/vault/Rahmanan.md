## Introduction

Raḥmānān (𐩧𐩢𐩣𐩬𐩬 in Musnad script) was the primary name for the monotheistic God worshipped in South Arabia from the fourth to sixth centuries CE, particularly within the Himyarite Kingdom. <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference> The name marked a pivotal transformation in Arabian religious history, as the ruling class of Himyar converted to Judaism and replaced their polytheistic invocations with references to this singular deity. <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference> Rahmanan represents a crucial bridge between ancient South Arabian religion, Abrahamic monotheism, and the eventual rise of Islam, as the term is widely considered the direct precursor to the Quranic epithet "al-Raḥmān."

---

## Etymology

- **Root meaning**: The name derives from the Semitic root *r-ḥ-m*, meaning "mercy" or "compassion." <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference> In Sabaic (the South Arabian language), "Raḥmānān" literally translates to "The Merciful."
- **Language of origin**: The South Arabian root is considered a loan from Jewish Aramaic (raḥmānā). <mcreference link="https://wikiislam.net/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arab_Religion_in_Islam" index="5">5</mcreference> The Sabaic form with the definite article suffix (-an) mirrors the Arabic al-Raḥmān, both meaning "The Merciful." <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference>
- **Variants and evolution**: The root r-ḥ-m appears across multiple Semitic languages:
  - Akkadian: *rēmēnȗ* (sometimes an epithet for Ninurta)
  - Hebrew: Appears in the Hebrew Bible
  - Old Aramaic: Especially as an epithet for the Mesopotamian god Hadad
  - The earliest known usage is in a polytheistic bilingual inscription from northeastern Syria (Akkadian and Aramaic) dedicated to Hadad, reading in Aramaic: "merciful god to whom prayer is sweet." <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference>
- **Symbolic implications**: The name embodies the central theological shift from multiple deities with specific domains to a single, all-encompassing deity characterized primarily by mercy and compassion.

> *"The Sabaic form Rahmanan is widely agreed to be the source of the Arabic form al-Raḥmān, especially as both forms use the definite article."* <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference>  
> — Modern scholarly consensus

## Cultural Context

- **Civilization**: The Himyarite Kingdom and broader South Arabian monotheistic communities <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyar" index="3">3</mcreference>
- **Geographic Region**: South Arabia, centered in modern-day Yemen, particularly the highlands around the ancient capital of Zafar and later Sana'a <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyar" index="3">3</mcreference>
- **Historical Period**: Predominantly 4th-6th centuries CE, with the theological framework continuing into the early Islamic period <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia" index="1">1</mcreference>
- **Alternative Names**:
  - al-Raḥmān (Arabic form) — The direct linguistic descendant used in the Quran
  - "Lord of Heaven" / "Lord of the Jews" / "Master of Heaven and Earth" — Epithets found in inscriptions <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference>

---

## Attributes and Domains

- **Domains of power**: Universal creator and sustainer, justice, mercy, providence over Israel and broader monotheistic communities
- **Symbols**: No physical representations; worship was aniconic (without images)
- **Sacred associations**: Heaven and Earth as primary domains
- **Elemental associations**: Cosmic order, celestial realm

| Attribute | Meaning |
|-----------|----------|
| The Merciful | Primary defining characteristic emphasizing compassion |
| Lord of Heaven | Celestial sovereignty and universal dominion |
| Lord of the Jews | Connection to the God of Israel and Jewish monotheism |
| Master of Heaven and Earth | Complete cosmic authority |

## Myths and Legends

**The Great Transformation**  
The most significant theological narrative surrounding Rahmanan is not a traditional myth but a historical-religious transformation. When the ruling class of Himyar converted to Judaism around 375-400 CE under King Malkikarib Yuhamin, public inscriptions underwent a dramatic shift. <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyar" index="3">3</mcreference> References to the traditional South Arabian pantheon—including Wadd, ʿAthtar, ʿAmm, and Almaqah—ceased entirely, replaced by invocations to Rahmanan as the sole deity. <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyar" index="3">3</mcreference> This transformation was so complete that by the fifth century, epigraphic evidence is nearly exclusively monotheistic. <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia" index="1">1</mcreference>

**Evolution from Kahl**  
Scholars propose that Rahmanan may have evolved from Kahl, a North Arabian deity who had already begun assimilating monotheistic characteristics between the 1st and 3rd centuries CE, partly attested in the 3rd-century Sabaic poem Zaid Inan 11. <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference> This suggests a gradual theological development rather than an abrupt replacement.

**Christian Reinterpretation**  
After the Aksumite Kingdom conquered Himyar in 530 CE, Rahmanan continued to be invoked but was reinterpreted within a Christian framework. During the reign of the Christian Himyarite king Sumyafa Ashwa, Jesus was referred to as "the son of Raḥmānān." <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference> Later, under King Abraha, Jesus was described as "the Messiah of Raḥmānān," and inscriptions appeared with the phrase "Rahmanan and his son Christ the conqueror." <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference>

## Worship and Rituals

- **Forms of worship**: Prayer (ṣlwth), invocations for blessings, justice, and children; inscriptions often ended with the Hebrew words "shalom" and "amen" <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyar" index="3">3</mcreference>
- **Prayer structure**: People prayed to Rahmanan for:
  - A life of justice (ḥyw ṣdq)
  - A death of justice (mwt ṣdq)
  - Healthy children who would "fight for the name of the Merciful"
  - Divine favor and wisdom <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference>
- **Sacred language**: Inscriptions were primarily in the Sabean language, with occasional use of Hebrew, particularly for religious formulae <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyar" index="3">3</mcreference>
- **Archaeological remains**: 
  - Ry 515 inscription: "By the Merciful, Lord of the Jews"
  - Ry 520 inscription: Extensive prayer invoking blessings from "the Merciful, Master of Heaven"
  - The Marib Dam inscription (CIH 541, dated 548 CE): References a priest, monastery, and abbot, with crosses and Trinitarian references alongside Rahmanan <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyar" index="3">3</mcreference>
  - The Jabal Dabub inscription: Contains a pre-Islamic version of the Basmala <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference>
  - A 2018 discovery in Yemen of a pre-Islamic Basmala reading: "In the name of Allah, Rahman; Rahman lord of the heavens" <mcreference link="https://wikiislam.net/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arab_Religion_in_Islam" index="5">5</mcreference>

## Later Influence

- **Syncretism**: The name Rahmanan was used to refer to different theological conceptions:
  - In Jewish contexts: Identified with YHWH, the God of Israel
  - In Christian contexts: The Father in Trinitarian theology, with Jesus as "the son of Rahmanan" or "the Messiah of Rahmanan"
  - Possible monolatrous interpretation: Some inscriptions (CIH 543) may suggest Himyar distinguished their high god (Rahmanan) from the high god of Israel, practicing monolatry rather than pure monotheism <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference>

- **Legacy in later religions**: 
  - The term al-Raḥmān became one of the most important names for God in Islam, appearing 57 times in the Quran
  - The Quranic Basmala ("In the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful") directly echoes the pre-Islamic South Arabian formula
  - The Quran identifies Allah and al-Raḥmān as one and the same (Quran 17:110)
  - Historical evidence suggests that in Muhammad's time, some Meccans perceived al-Rahman as a foreign deity, distinct from Allah, reflecting the regional religious divisions of pre-Islamic Arabia <mcreference link="https://wikiislam.net/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arab_Religion_in_Islam" index="5">5</mcreference>

- **Presence in modern literature and culture**: Rahmanan serves as a crucial example in academic discussions of:
  - The development of monotheism in Arabia
  - The religious transformations preceding Islam
  - The complex interplay between Judaism, Christianity, and indigenous Arabian beliefs
  - The linguistic and theological continuities between pre-Islamic and Islamic Arabia

---

## Modern Interpretations

- **Comparative mythology**: Rahmanan represents a unique case of monotheistic development outside the direct influence of Mediterranean Christianity or Rabbinic Judaism, termed by scholars as "gentile monotheism," "Himyarite monotheism," or "Rahmanism" <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia" index="1">1</mcreference>

- **Recent academic theories**: 
  - The transition from polytheism to monotheism in South Arabia was remarkably swift, with the last polytheistic inscription dating to the 380s CE <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia" index="1">1</mcreference>
  - Rahmanan is mentioned 58 times in surviving Late Sabaic inscriptions, none showing evidence of pagan or polytheistic context <mcreference link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahmanan" index="4">4</mcreference>
  - The theological framework of Rahmanism may have provided a middle ground for populations transitioning between different monotheistic traditions
  - The 2018 discovery of a pre-Islamic Basmala inscription demonstrates the direct continuity between South Arabian Rahmanism and Islamic theology

- **Linguistic discoveries**: Professor Ahmad al-Jallad's work has established that:
  - The name raḥmān appears in numerous South Arabian pre-Islamic inscriptions derived from Jewish Aramaic
  - The use of Rahman became truly monotheistic only in the sixth century CE, previously existing in a monolatric context
  - The Quran shows a chronological progression in divine names: Rabb (Lord) in the earliest phase, then al-Rahman, and later almost exclusive use of Allah <mcreference link="https://wikiislam.net/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arab_Religion_in_Islam" index="5">5</mcreference>
