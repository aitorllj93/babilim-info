## Introduction

Magos (also rendered as Magus) is a primordial deity in Phoenician cosmology, known primarily through the fragmented theogony preserved by Philo of Byblos and attributed to the ancient Phoenician writer Sanchuniathon. As a second-generation deity born from the mysterious Wanderers (or Titans), Magos represents a crucial link in the genealogical chain that connects the primordial chaos to the establishment of cosmic order. Together with his consort Amunos, Magos fathered Sydyk ("Righteousness") and Misor ("Justice"), who are credited with discovering the use of salt and establishing fundamental principles of cosmic balance.

---

## Etymology

- **Root meaning**: The name "Magos" (Greek: Μάγος) may derive from ancient Semitic roots, though its precise etymology remains uncertain. The Greek form suggests a possible connection to wisdom, knowledge, or mystical understanding.
- **Language of origin**: Phoenician, preserved through Greek translation
- **Variants and evolution**: The name appears in manuscripts as both "Magos" and "Magus," reflecting variations in Greek transliteration of the original Phoenician name. The Greek form became standardized in later classical sources.
- **Symbolic or metaphoric implications**: The name may carry connotations of generative power and primordial wisdom, befitting a deity who bridges the chaotic Titans and the ordered generation of justice deities.

> _"Sydyk is listed together with Misor, the two being credited as the first to use salt. They are described as being born from Amunos and Magos who were in turn born from the Wanderers or Titans."_  
> — Philo of Byblos, via Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica

## Cultural Context

- **Civilization**: Phoenician
- **Geographic Region**: Eastern Mediterranean coast (modern-day Lebanon, Syria, and northern Israel)
- **Historical Period**: The mythological narrative is attributed to Sanchuniathon, allegedly predating the Trojan War (c. 1200 BCE), though the actual composition likely dates to the Hellenistic period (4th-1st century BCE)
- **Alternative Names**:

  - The exact Phoenician form of the name is uncertain due to the loss of original Phoenician texts

## Myths and Legends

**The Generation of Justice**

In the Phoenician theogony preserved by Sanchuniathon, Magos and [[Amunos]] were born from the Wanderers (also called Titans), representing the second generation of cosmic beings. These primordial entities existed in the transition between the formless chaos and the establishment of divine order. From their union came [[Sydyk]] and [[Misor]], the twin principles of righteousness and justice, who made the crucial discovery of salt—a substance fundamental to preservation, purification, and covenant-making in ancient Near Eastern cultures.

> _"From these were born Amunos and Magos, who dwelt in Phœnicia, and they brought forth the generation of Suduc and Misor—these are the inventors of salt."_  
> — Sanchuniathon, via Philo of Byblos, preserved in Eusebius

**The Descent from the Wanderers**

Magos's birth from the Wanderers or Titans places him within the cosmic genealogy that begins with the primordial chaos and wind, proceeding through various stages of cosmic organization. This genealogical position suggests that Magos represents a crucial stage in the evolution from pure elemental forces to anthropomorphic deities associated with ethical and social principles.

## Later Influence

- **Syncretism**: The theogonic structure preserving Magos shows influence from or parallels with Greek cosmogonic traditions, particularly Hesiod's Theogony, though the specific correspondences for Magos remain unclear.
- **Legacy in later religions**: The concept of divine genealogies leading from chaos to order influenced Hellenistic philosophical and religious thought, though Magos himself did not maintain a prominent position in later traditions.
- **Presence in modern literature and culture**: Magos remains an obscure figure primarily of interest to scholars of ancient Near Eastern religion and comparative mythology.

## Modern Interpretations

- **Euhemerist Reading**: Sanchuniathon's account is explicitly euhemerist—the theory that gods were originally mortal humans (kings, heroes, priests) who were later deified. In this interpretation, **Magos may represent an ancient priestly class or priest-king** (the name possibly connecting to "magician" or "wise one"), while **[[Amunos]] may represent kingship or royal authority**. Their "offspring" [[Sydyk]] and [[Misor]] (Righteousness and Justice) would then symbolize the legal and ethical systems established by these early rulers. According to this view, the Phoenicians "applied the names of their kings to the elements of the cosmos" and worshipped deified natural forces and cultural heroes rather than purely supernatural beings.

- **Psychological/archetypal readings**: Magos can be understood as representing the archetypal transition from primordial, undifferentiated cosmic forces to the emergence of ethical principles (through his offspring Sydyk and Misor). This reflects a universal mythological pattern of cosmic evolution from chaos to moral order. Whether understood as cosmic forces or deified humans, the pattern remains consistent.

- **Comparative mythology**: The genealogical structure mirrors patterns found in Greek (Titans to Olympians), Mesopotamian (primordial gods to cosmic order), and other ancient Near Eastern cosmogonies. The pairing with Amunos may reflect the common mythological motif of divine couples generating the next stage of cosmic development—or, in euhemerist terms, the historical alliance between priestly and royal powers that established early Phoenician civilization.

- **Recent academic theories or discoveries**: The 20th-century discovery of Ugaritic texts confirmed many elements of the Sanchuniathon fragments, lending credibility to the preservation of genuine ancient Phoenician mythological material, even if filtered through Hellenistic interpretations and euhemerist philosophy. However, specific information about Magos beyond the basic genealogical framework remains limited. Modern scholars recognize that Sanchuniathon's work likely blends authentic Phoenician traditions with Hellenistic philosophical interpretations, particularly euhemerism.
