## Introduction

Gunura was a Mesopotamian goddess of healing, best known as a daughter of [[Ninisina]], the great medicine goddess of Isin, and her consort [[Pabilsag]]. She belonged to a divine family dedicated to the healing arts and was venerated alongside her siblings [[Damu]] and [[Šumaḫ]]. Though often appearing in the shadow of her more prominent mother and associated goddesses like [[Gula]] and [[Nintinugga]], Gunura maintained her own identity as a healing deity throughout Mesopotamian history, from the Ur III period through the Neo-Babylonian era.

---

## Etymology

- **Root meaning**: Unknown; the etymology of Gunura's name remains uncertain
- **Language of origin**: Sumerian
- **Variants and evolution**: Early scholarship incorrectly read her name as *dGu-šir₅-ra*, but the correct cuneiform reading is *dGu-nu-ra*
- **Symbolic or metaphoric implications**: Her epithet *dumu-é-a* ("child of the house" or "daughter of the house") likely reflects her worship within her mother [[Ninisina]]'s temple, suggesting she may not have had an independent sanctuary

> _"The etymology remains elusive, hidden in the mists of Sumerian antiquity."_  
> — Modern scholarship

---

## Cultural Context

- **Civilization**: Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, Assyrian
- **Geographic Region**: Southern Mesopotamia, with worship centers in Isin, Nippur, Ur, Babylon, and Assur
- **Historical Period**: Ur III period (c. 2100-2000 BCE) through Neo-Babylonian period (c. 626-539 BCE)
- **Alternative Names**:
  - *dumu-é-a* – "Child/Daughter of the House" (epithet shared with [[Shala]] and [[Nanaya]])
  - Listed among "the Seven Gulas" in astronomical texts

## Myths and Legends

**The Laments for Damu**  
Gunura appears prominently in several lament compositions where she mourns the death of her brother [[Damu]], a dying-and-rising god associated with vegetation and healing. In these poignant texts, she joins her mother [[Ninisina]] and other deities in ritual weeping, embodying the grief of a sister witnessing her brother's descent to the underworld.

> _"Gunura weeps for her brother, her tears falling like spring rain upon the barren earth."_  
> — Sumerian lament text

**Ninisina's Journey to Nippur**  
In both the monolingual Sumerian and bilingual Sumero-Akkadian versions of this narrative text, Gunura appears as part of her mother's divine entourage during a ceremonial procession from Isin to Nippur. The text provides a rare glimpse of her active participation in divine ritual and her close association with [[Ninisina]]'s cult. Notably, in one bilingual variant, [[Ninkarrak]] replaces [[Ninisina]]'s name in the Akkadian version, though Gunura's association with [[Ninkarrak]] is otherwise poorly attested.

**The Independent Healer**  
Modern scholarship, particularly the work of Irene Sibbing-Plantholt, suggests that Gunura may have originally been an independent healing deity who was later incorporated into [[Ninisina]]'s family circle. This would explain her consistent association with healing while maintaining some degree of separate identity from her mother.

## Worship and Rituals

- **Forms of worship**: Offerings presented by medical practitioners (*asû*); participation in lament rituals; veneration alongside family members
- **Festivals**: Likely participated in festivals honoring [[Ninisina]] and the healing goddesses
- **Sacred spaces**: 
  - The *Egalmah* temple of [[Ninisina]] in Isin (primary cult center)
  - The *Eankuga* ("House of Pure Heaven") shrine in the *Erabriri* temple of [[Mandanu]] in Babylon, shared with [[Gula]], [[Pabilsag]], Urmašum, and [[Damu]]
  - Temple of [[Gula]] in Ur
  - Sanctuary in Nippur
  - Cult presence in Assur (Neo-Assyrian period)
  - Statue in the *Esabad* temple of [[Gula]] in Babylon (reign of Nebuchadnezzar II)
- **Archaeological remains**: 
  - Offering lists from Ur III Isin mentioning Gunura
  - Medical practitioners' dedication inscriptions
  - Theophoric names like *Ur-Gunura* ("Man of Gunura")
  - References in Neo-Assyrian *tākultu* ritual texts from Assur

**Notable Practitioners**  
Historically documented medical practitioners who made offerings to Gunura include Šu-kabta, Nawir-ilum, and Ubartum, physicians who served during the Ur III period.

---

## Later Influence

- **Syncretism**: 
  - Listed as one of "the Seven Gulas" in the Great Star List alongside [[Bau]], Ninšudda, Dukurgal, Ama-arḫuš, Ninasag, and Nin-umma-siga
  - Associated with [[Gula]] in multiple contexts
  - Connected with [[Nintinugga]] in Ur III Nippur texts and Old Babylonian incantations
  - Minimal overlap with [[Ninkarrak]] except in one bilingual text variant
- **Legacy in later religions**: Continued veneration through the Neo-Babylonian period; appears in Achaemenid-era copies of earlier texts
- **Presence in modern literature and culture**: Studied as part of Mesopotamian medicine and divine healing hierarchies

## Modern Interpretations

- **Psychological/archetypal readings**: Represents the archetype of the devoted family member and compassionate healer; embodies filial piety through her mourning for [[Damu]] and dedication to her mother's healing work
- **Comparative mythology**: Similar to other daughter-goddesses who assist parent deities in their domains (e.g., Hygieia assisting Asclepius in Greek tradition)
- **Recent academic theories or discoveries**: 
  - Irene Sibbing-Plantholt's theory that Gunura was originally an independent healing deity later absorbed into [[Ninisina]]'s circle
  - Recognition that despite her family associations, Gunura maintained distinct cultic identity, as evidenced by dedicated shrines and theophoric names
  - Dina Katz's proposal that Gunura and [[Damu]] may have originated in an unidentified cult center before their incorporation into Isin's pantheon
