## Introduction
Huba (also known as Khuba) was an important goddess in the Urartian pantheon, primarily known as the divine consort of Theispas, the god of storms and thunder. While not as extensively documented as some other Urartian deities, her position as the partner of one of the three principal gods of Urartu indicates her significant status within their religious hierarchy.

---

## Etymology

- **Root meaning**: The exact etymology of Huba's name remains uncertain
- **Language of origin**: Urartian, possibly with Hurrian influences
- **Variants and evolution**: Also recorded as Khuba in some sources

---

## Cultural Context

- **Civilization**: Urartu (Kingdom of Van)
- **Geographic Region**: Armenian Highlands (present-day eastern Turkey, Armenia, and northwestern Iran)
- **Historical Period**: 9th to 6th century BCE
- **Alternative Names**: Khuba

---

## Attributes and Domains

- **Domains of power**: Likely associated with aspects of storms, fertility, and nurturing
- **Symbols**: Unknown, though possibly shared storm imagery with Theispas
- **Elemental associations**: Likely water and air, given her connection to the storm god

---

## Myths and Legends

Few specific myths about Huba have survived. Her primary identity in surviving records is as the consort of [[Teisheba]], the powerful storm god who formed part of the supreme trinity of Urartian deities alongside [[Haldi]] and [[Shivini]].

The Urartian religion was heavily influenced by neighboring cultures, particularly Mesopotamian, Hurrian, and Hittite religious traditions. The relationship between Huba and Teisheba may have paralleled other divine couples in Near Eastern mythology, such as the Hurrian Teshub and Hebat.

---

## Worship and Rituals

The religious practices of Urartu are known primarily through archaeological evidence and inscriptions. A significant inscription discovered near the capital Tushpa (Van) from the 9th century BCE lists 79 gods and the sacrifices to be made to each. As the consort of Teisheba, Huba would have received offerings as part of the state religion.

Urartian religious ceremonies involved animal sacrifices, with the number and type of animals sacrificed depending on the deity's rank in the pantheon. These rituals took place both in temples and in open-air settings.

---

## Later Influence

With the fall of Urartu in the 6th century BCE to the Medes, many aspects of Urartian culture and religion were absorbed into later Armenian traditions or lost to history. The specific legacy of Huba in later religious developments is difficult to trace due to limited surviving records.

---

## Modern Interpretations

Modern scholarly interest in Urartian religion has grown with archaeological discoveries in the Armenian Highlands. As a female deity in the Urartian pantheon, Huba represents an important aspect of understanding gender roles and divine partnerships in ancient Near Eastern religions.
