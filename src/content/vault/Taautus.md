## Introduction

Taautus of Byblos stands as one of the most significant figures in Phoenician mythology, revered as the primordial inventor of writing and the foundation of all human learning. More than merely a deity, Taautus represents the transformative power of the written word—the moment when human thought transcended the fleeting nature of speech and became permanent, transmissible across time and space. The Phoenicians recognized him as the divine scribe who first conceived the alphabet, while the Egyptians knew him as [[Thoth]], and the Greeks as [[Hermes]]. His legacy bridges cultures and civilizations, embodying the universal human quest for knowledge and wisdom.

---

## Etymology

- **Root meaning**: The name Taautus (Greek: Τααυτος, Taautos) derives from the Egyptian *Ḏḥwty* (Djehuty), meaning "He who is like the ibis"
- **Language of origin**: Egyptian, transmitted through Phoenician and rendered into Greek
- **Variants and evolution**: 
  - Egyptian: Ḏḥwty, Djehuty, Tehuti
  - Coptic: Ⲑⲱⲟⲩⲧ (Thōout)
  - Greek: Thoth (Θώθ), Hermes when syncretized
  - Phoenician rendering: Taautus, Taautos
- **Symbolic implications**: The ibis, sacred to Thoth, was associated with wisdom due to its curved beak resembling the crescent moon and its methodical behavior. The name thus connects the deity to celestial knowledge, measurement, and the recording of time.

> _"Sanchuniathon realized that Taautos was the first person under the sun who thought of the invention of writing and who began to compose records, thereby laying the foundation, as it were, of learning."_  
> — Philo of Byblos, as cited by Eusebius, *Praeparatio Evangelica*

## Cultural Context

- **Civilization**: Phoenician, with Egyptian origins
- **Geographic Region**: Byblos (modern-day Lebanon), with connections to Egypt
- **Historical Period**: Mythological origins in pre-dynastic times; literary attestation from the Hellenistic period (c. 2nd century CE through Philo of Byblos)
- **Alternative Names**:
  - **Thouth** – Egyptian name as recorded by Philo of Byblos
  - **Thoth** – Alexandrian Greek form
  - **Hermes** – Greek interpretatio graeca
  - **Djehuty** – Ancient Egyptian pronunciation

## Myths and Legends

**The Invention of Writing**  
According to the Phoenician historian Sanchuniathon (as preserved by Philo of Byblos), Taautus was the first being to conceive of writing. He discovered the art of forming letters and creating permanent records, transforming human consciousness by enabling knowledge to transcend individual lifetimes. This invention made him the foundation of all learning, and subsequent generations venerated him as the divine source of wisdom and education.

> _"He did this since he realized that Taautos was the first person under the sun who thought of the invention of writing and who began to compose records, thereby laying the foundation, as it were, of learning. The Egyptians call him Thouth and the Alexandrians Thoth and the Greeks translated his name as Hermes."_  
> — Philo of Byblos, *Phoenician Matters*, fragment preserved by Eusebius

**The Divine Scribe and Chronicler**  
Taautus served as the recorder of divine events and cosmic knowledge. According to Phoenician tradition, the Cabeiri (Kabeiroi) and [[Eshmun]] (Asclepius) wrote accounts of events under Taautus's direction, establishing him as the patron of all who record and preserve knowledge.

**The Nature of the Dragon**  
Taautus regarded the nature of the dragon and serpents as divine, declaring them "of all reptiles most full of breath, and fiery." This teaching was adopted by both Phoenicians and Egyptians, connecting Taautus to serpent wisdom and the vital breath of life.

**Inheritance of Egypt**  
In Phoenician cosmogony, when [[Cronus]] (El) distributed territories among the gods, he bequeathed Egypt to Taautus, recognizing the deity's special connection to that land—a mythological acknowledgment of the Egyptian origins of Thoth-Taautus.

## Worship and Rituals

- **Forms of worship**: 
  - Veneration as patron of scribes and scholars
  - Offerings made before writing or learning activities
  - Consultation for wisdom and arbitration in disputes
  - Recognition as the divine source of magical knowledge
  
- **Festivals**: While specific Phoenician festivals to Taautus are not well-documented, his Egyptian counterpart Thoth was honored during:
  - The Festival of Thoth (first month of the Egyptian calendar)
  - Celebrations associated with the lunar cycles
  
- **Sacred spaces**: 
  - In Phoenician Byblos, where he was particularly venerated
  - The great Temple of Thoth at Hermopolis (Khemenu) in Egypt, the primary cult center
  - Steles and temples erected by the Phoenicians honoring their cultural heroes
  
- **Archaeological remains**: 
  - Inscriptions at Hermopolis calling Thoth "The Lord of Eshmun," showing Phoenician-Egyptian syncretism
  - Massive statues of Thoth as a baboon at Hermopolis
  - Obelisk of Nectanebo II dedicated to "Thoth Thrice-Great, Lord of Hermopolis" (British Museum)
  - Temple remains at el-Ashmunein (ancient Hermopolis)

## Later Influence

- **Syncretism**: 
  - Identified completely with Egyptian [[Thoth]]
  - Equated with Greek [[Hermes]] through *interpretatio graeca*
  - Combined as [[Hermes Trismegistus]] ("Thrice-Great Hermes") in Hellenistic Egypt
  - Associated with the Phoenician deity [[Eshmun]]
  
- **Legacy in later religions**:
  - In Hermeticism, became the legendary author of the *Hermetica*
  - Christian writers (Lactantius, Augustine, Marsilio Ficino) considered Hermes Trismegistus a wise pagan prophet who foresaw Christianity
  - Islamic tradition associates Hermes Trismegistus with the prophet Idris (Biblical Enoch)
  - Baháʼí writings also reference this syncretic figure
  
- **Presence in modern literature and culture**:
  - Central figure in Western esoteric traditions and occultism
  - The Hermetic tradition, alchemy, and astrology trace their origins to his teachings
  - Continues to symbolize the archetype of the divine teacher and scribe

## Modern Interpretations

- **Psychological/archetypal readings**: 
  - Represents the archetype of the Magus or Wise Old Man in Jungian psychology
  - Embodies the human drive to preserve and transmit knowledge
  - Symbolizes the transition from oral to written culture
  
- **Comparative mythology**: 
  - Parallels with [[Odin]]'s discovery of runes in Norse mythology
  - Similar to Nabu in Mesopotamian tradition (god of writing and wisdom)
  - Echoes in the figure of [[Enki]]/Ea as the god of wisdom and magic
  - Connections to Chinese cultural heroes like Cangjie, inventor of Chinese characters
  
- **Recent academic theories**:
  - Philo of Byblos's account (early 2nd century CE) preserves genuine Phoenician mythological elements
  - Ugaritic texts discovered at Ras Shamra (1929) confirmed many details from Sanchuniathon's narrative
  - Modern scholarship sees Taautus as a euhemerized version of Thoth, where an Egyptian deity was reinterpreted as a historical Phoenician culture hero
  - The syncretism reflects the deep cultural exchange between Phoenician and Egyptian civilizations
