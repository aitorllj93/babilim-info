## Introduction
Ḫepat was one of the most important goddesses in the ancient Near East, particularly revered in the Hurrian pantheon. She was the supreme goddess and divine consort of the weather god Teshub, forming the head of the divine family. Originally associated with the city of Halab (modern Aleppo) in northern Syria, her worship spread throughout Anatolia and the Levant, influencing multiple cultures including the Hittites and Luwians. As a powerful maternal figure and "Queen of Heaven," Ḫepat embodied divine authority and protection.

---

## Etymology

- **Root meaning** of the name: Likely derived from *ḫa-a-ba-du* (Ḫalabatu), meaning "she of Ḫalab (Aleppo)"
- **Language of origin**: Originally Syrian, later adopted into Hurrian
- **Variants and evolution**: Also written as Ḫebat, Hepat, or Hepa; appears in theophoric names as -hepa or -heba
- **Symbolic or metaphoric implications**: An alternative etymology connects her name to the root *ḫbb*, meaning "to love"

> _"The name Ḫalabatu is most likely a nisba, indication of point of origin, meaning 'she of Halab.'"_  
> — Alfonso Archi, Hurrian scholar

##  Cultural Context

- **Civilization**: Originally Syrian, primarily associated with Hurrian culture
- **Geographic Region**: Northern Syria, Anatolia, and parts of Mesopotamia
- **Historical Period**: Third millennium BCE through first millennium BCE
- **Alternative Names**:
  - Ḫebat – Common variant spelling
  - Ḫalabatu – Early Eblaite form of her name
  - Hepa/Heba – Form used in theophoric names

## Attributes and Domains

- **Domains of power**: Divine authority, royalty, motherhood, protection
- **Symbols**: Throne, lion (her sacred animal)
- **Sacred animals**: Lion
- **Elemental associations**: Possibly solar aspects (through later syncretism)

## Myths and Legends

**Main Myth or Story**  
While no complete myths focusing solely on Ḫepat have survived, she features prominently in the Hurrian mythological cycle involving her husband Teshub. In these stories, she appears as the steadfast divine queen supporting Teshub in his conflicts with Kumarbi, the former king of the gods.

**Other Myths**  
- **Divine Family** – Ḫepat and Teshub were parents to several divine children, including their son Šarruma and daughters Allanzu and Kunzišalli
- **Allašši Ceremony** – A ritual dedicated to Ḫepat concerned with the concept of "ladyship," analogous to Teshub's ceremony of "kingship"

---

## Worship and Rituals

- **Forms of worship**: Offerings of gold and silver objects, cattle sacrifices, throne worship
- **Festivals**: The allašši ceremony celebrating her divine queenship
- **Sacred spaces**: Major temples in Kummanni and Lawazantiya in Kizzuwatna; originally worshiped in Halab (Aleppo)
- **Archaeological remains**: Depicted on the central relief of the Hittite Yazilikaya sanctuary, where she appears alongside Teshub and their divine family

---

## Later Influence

- **Syncretism**: In Hittite religion, sometimes identified with the Sun goddess of Arinna
- **Legacy in later religions**: Possibly connected to the later Lydian goddess Hipta known from Orphic sources
- **Presence in modern culture**: Her image at Yazilikaya remains one of the most significant representations of ancient Near Eastern goddesses

## Modern Interpretations

- **Comparative mythology**: Ḫepat represents a common pattern of divine couples in ancient Near Eastern religions, where a supreme weather god is paired with a powerful goddess
- **Recent academic theories**: Studies suggest Ḫepat's elevation to supreme goddess status in western Hurrian areas represents a regional theological development, as she displaced the goddess Šauška from this role in eastern Hurrian centers
