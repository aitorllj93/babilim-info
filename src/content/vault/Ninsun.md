## Introduction

Ninsun (Sumerian: Nin-sumun(ak), cuneiform: 𒀭𒊩𒌆𒄢) was a prominent Sumerian goddess revered as the divine mother of the legendary hero Gilgamesh and wife of the deified king Lugalbanda. Known primarily as the "Lady of the Wild Cows," she embodied maternal wisdom, dream interpretation, and the nurturing qualities that ancient Mesopotamian herdsmen sought in their cattle. Her role in the Epic of Gilgamesh and her worship across major Mesopotamian cities established her as one of the most beloved maternal deities of ancient Sumer.

---

## Etymology

- **Root meaning**: The name Ninsun derives from the Sumerian "Nin-sumun(ak)," where "nin" means "lady" and "sumun" (also read as "sun") means "wild cow"
- **Language of origin**: Sumerian
- **Variants and evolution**: The cuneiform sign GUL could be read as both "sun" and "sumun" in Sumerian, with identical meanings. The name was initially understood as "lady wild cow" but later interpreted as a genitive construction meaning "lady of the wild cows" (Ninsumun or Ninsumuna)
- **Symbolic implications**: The bovine connection emphasized her maternal, nurturing nature and her role as "the flawless cow," "a mother of good offspring that loves the offspring," embodying the ideal qualities herdsmen desired in their livestock

> _"She was the 'flawless cow' and a 'mother of good offspring that loves the offspring.'"_  
> — Britannica Encyclopedia

## Cultural Context

- **Civilization**: Sumerian/Mesopotamian
- **Geographic Region**: Southern Mesopotamia, particularly the city-state of Lagash and surrounding regions
- **Historical Period**: Early Dynastic IIIa period (c. 2600-2350 BCE) through the Seleucid period (312-63 BCE)
- **Alternative Names**:
  - Ninsumun – more accurate modern reading
  - Ninsumuna – variant form
  - Rimāt-Ninsun – Akkadian epithet meaning "wild cow Ninsun"
  - Lamma-Ninsumuna – in Lagash texts, indicating her protective function

## Attributes and Domains

- **Domains of power**: Motherhood, dream interpretation, wisdom, fertility, protection of heroes and kings, nurturing
- **Symbols**: Wild cow, bovine imagery
- **Sacred animals**: Wild cow (her primary sacred animal)

## Myths and Legends

**Mother of Gilgamesh**  
Ninsun's most famous role appears in the Epic of Gilgamesh, where she serves as the divine mother of the hero. In the Standard Babylonian version, she demonstrates her wisdom by interpreting Gilgamesh's prophetic dreams about his future companion Enkidu. When Gilgamesh dreams of a falling star from heaven that he cannot lift, Ninsun explains: "A strong partner shall come to you, one who can save the life of a friend. He will be the most powerful in strength of arms in the land. His strength will be as great as that of the sky-bolt of Anu. You will love him as a wife, you will dote upon him."

She also pleads with the sun-god [[Shamash]] to protect her son on his dangerous journey, demonstrating her maternal devotion. Upon meeting Enkidu, she formally adopts him as her son, treating him with the same care as Gilgamesh.

> _"Son of Lugalbanda, Gilgamesh, perfect in strength, / Son of the lofty cow, the wild cow Ninsun."_  
> — Epic of Gilgamesh

**Marriage to Lugalbanda**  
An Early Dynastic literary text from Tell Abu Salabikh recounts the marriage between the legendary king [[Lugalbanda]] and Ninsun, though the text is poorly preserved and difficult to interpret. In this myth, the Anunna gods collectively arrange the details of her marriage, playing the role typically assigned to parents of the bride.

**Other Myths**  
- **Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven** – Ninsun appears in some copies of this Sumerian composition
- **Mother of Dumuzi** – A sparsely attested tradition regarded her as the mother of the dying god [[Dumuzi]], indicating conflation with his usual mother Duttur

## Worship and Rituals

- **Forms of worship**: Offerings during lunar festivals, prayers for protection and guidance, dream interpretation consultations
- **Festivals**: Lunar festivals in Nippur during the Ur III period; New Year festival of Ishtar in Seleucid Uruk
- **Sacred spaces**: 
  - **KI.KALki** – her main cult center
  - **E-mah ("Magnificent Temple")** in Ur, built by Ur-Nammu (r. 2011-2094 BCE)
  - **Egalmah ("Exalted Palace")** in Uruk (later associated with [[Gula]])
  - Temples in Lagash, Nippur, Umma, and other southern Mesopotamian cities
- **Archaeological remains**: A fragmentary stone relief at the Louvre Museum possibly depicts Ninsun seated on a throne, wearing the flounced robe (a marker of divinity) with long hair

---

## Later Influence

- **Syncretism**: Equated with the healing goddess [[Gula]], especially in syncretic hymns; also associated with [[Baba]] and Gatumdu through close connections
- **Legacy in later religions**: Kings of the Third Dynasty of Ur (particularly Ur-Nammu and Shulgi) claimed Ninsun and Lugalbanda as their divine parents and Gilgamesh as their brother to legitimize their rule over Mesopotamia
- **Presence in modern literature and culture**: Appears in modern retellings of the Epic of Gilgamesh and studies of Mesopotamian mythology

## Modern Interpretations

- **Psychological/archetypal readings**: Represents the archetypal wise mother who guides heroes through prophetic wisdom and unconditional love
- **Comparative mythology**: Shares characteristics with other ancient mother goddesses such as [[Ninhursag]] (Akkadian: Belit-ili) and [[Ninlil]] (Belit), representing the nurturing maternal divine feminine
- **Recent academic theories**: Modern scholarship has corrected the earlier reading "Ninsun" to the more accurate "Ninsumun" based on cuneiform analysis (Wilcke 1998-2001)
